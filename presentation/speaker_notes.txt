Robust Multi-Position Parametric Room Equalization
CSE 5403 — 17 minutes of planned speaking time

1. Title — 0:40
Introduce the engineering problem and the optimization method. The study
uses measured room responses and fits a single equalizer for a listening
area. The three main outcomes concern stationarity, spatial transfer, and
the effect of warm starts.

2. Research question — 1:10
Point out how the response curves differ before correction. A shared
filter applies the same dB correction at each frequency to all positions.
Define single-seat, mean, and robust EQ. The smoke-test curves show
the kind of response change we evaluate.

3. Measurements — 1:30
Describe one source and the 5 cm grid. Explain the 25 cm gap between
eligible training positions and the 126-point test strip. The three
seeds provide nested measurement sets. The center anchor stays fixed
and sets the level reference.

4. Formulation — 1:50
Define the seat loss, the log-mean-exp term, and gain regularization.
The smooth term approaches the largest training loss at small
temperature. Each biquad supplies gain, log center frequency, and log Q.
Explain the individual gain bounds and the distinction between those
bounds and the measured combined boost.

5. Analytical benchmark — 1:30
Derive the variance decomposition by expanding around the seat mean.
The unrestricted shared correction can remove the mean-response term.
The remaining floor is on training pooled RMSE, with the filter
parameterization and regularizer relaxed. The gap to that floor includes
the consequences of those relaxations and local optimization.

6. Nonlinear optimization — 2:10
Walk through the shared Jacobian and the seat-gradient covariance.
Explain why the curvature approximation is positive semidefinite.
Describe active variables, damping, clipping, and the projected Armijo
check. The projected-gradient norm is shared across the two solvers;
curvature evaluations are recorded separately.

7. Solver results — 1:40
Compare the evaluation medians within each objective. All matched
starts reached the common tolerance. Point to the different end values:
the faster solver by this measure did not necessarily reach the lowest
objective. The curves use best observed values as references.

8. Acoustic results — 1:15
Read the no-EQ and mean-EQ values as an example of the held-out
improvement. Explain pooled versus worst-seat RMSE. The center-seat
baseline performed well on this strip, so adding seats within the
training region did not guarantee an improvement here.

9. Spatial generalization — 1:30
Each line connects nested counts for one seed. Read negative values as
improvements over mean EQ. Contrast the frequency of training and
held-out improvements, then give their median magnitudes. These
comparisons come from repeated training splits on the same test strip.

10. Temperature ablation — 1:20
The pilot tried three geometric schedules on training subsets and
selected 32 to 8 to 2. The early stages share the same overall call cap
with the last stage. Every fit meets the threshold, and continuation
trades additional calls for a lower final objective on one start.

11. Initialization ablation — 1:10
Explain how the peaks come from the median training response. Compare
against random centers with the same gains and Q. There is a consistent
initial improvement, but the final objective is mixed. This suggests
that the initial objective is an incomplete predictor of where a local
solver will finish.

12. Conclusions — 1:15
Summarize the acoustic and optimization results. The robust objective
did help some worst training seats, while transfer to this holdout was
weak. State the one-source, one-room, and magnitude-only limits.
Refer to the report and reproducibility bundle for the exact splits and
raw traces.
